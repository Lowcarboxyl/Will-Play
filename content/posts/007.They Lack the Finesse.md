It’s the first day of Japanese full-immersion language class. I’m surrounded by a Malaysian, a Russian, three Mongolians, a Bolivian, a Spaniard, a German, two Hong Kongers, and 10 Chinese nationals. When the teacher starts to introduce everyone, I get to stand up proudly and say “WaTASHI wA A-MEh-RI-kA JiN deSU!” The first question I get—and which any person who has traveled to a foreign country will know well: “So… what do you think about Trump?”

Something in it—his complaints of America—are still something you could hear out of the mouth of a recently graduated UIUC Chinese grad student today. This interview was happening in a very specific time in the global Cold War. The stories of the American Red Scare and hiding under desks were indeed true, but it was a much more concrete conflict in other parts of the world. In 1962, the Cuban Missile Crisis made both the US and the USSR realize that a direct clash would result in true world annihilation. However, the stalemate did not stop, and instead moved to smaller countries. In March 1965, the first US ground combat troops landed in Vietnam.

And Vietnam was not alone. In January 1961, the CIA orchestrated the destabilization of a growing Republic of the Congo. In April of 1965, 22,000 US troops were sent to invade the Dominican Republic, once again due to fear of it joining the USSR. It is in this intensely fractured world where this specific interview with Lee Kuan Yew, Prime Minister of Singapore, took place.

In September 1963, after over 100 years under British rule, Singapore joined the greater Malaysia with many of the other former British colonial possessions in the region. Short story: it didn’t go well, and they were ousted forcefully from Malaysia in August 1965. At that time, the new Republic of Singapore was under threat of bombardment and blockade from all sides, protected only by their previous colonizers, the British navy and army. It is at this time, 22 days after the forceful expulsion, when Lee Kuan Yew gave an interview to Australian and British news. In it, he revealed that 5 years prior, an American CIA agent was imprisoned for bribery, but the case was not prosecuted. In return, the Americans offered Yew a 10 million dollar bribe, but it was refused. (That's Singaporean dollars—3.3 million USD at the time).

With that covered, please take a look at the interview! This British educated Singaporean can talk.

Under threat of becoming the local 29-year-old neoliberal supply-chain maximizer guy, let me be clear. I am not a Lee Kuan Yew stan. Aside from building a world trade capital in a place that's WAY too hot, I acknowledge his downsides. Instead, I’d like to focus particularly on how this small city-state viewed America at the time, and how this same theory still stands to this day.

Pulling out the bribery incident 5 years later is what we call in the business a big-brain play. By bringing up this transgression during a worldwide interview, he wanted to establish Singapore as a global player who would not settle as a pawn in the Cold War. After this moment, Lee Kuan Yew sent his second-in-command to create a “Non-Aligned” club of nations across Asia and Africa, while at the same time using the British military as their bodyguards.

However, while many of these other non-aligned countries tried to make non-alignment their own ideology, Lee Kuan Yew went the other way. He didn’t hate American money or people; rather, he hated the clumsy American state apparatus that tried to push around countries from the top down. He wanted to bring to Singapore what he (and the rest of the world) saw as the best of American business: efficient, non-aligned, radical transparency. So, over the next few years, instead of backroom CIA money for “economic development,” Lee Kuan Yew appealed to Silicon Valley boardrooms. They invited Texas Instruments, Hewlett-Packard, and major oil refineries to set up massive physical infrastructure on Singaporean land. They made it impossible for the American state to bully them, because the only thing stronger than the American state is the American dollar.

And that’s what I think strikes me about the interview. When Lee Kuan Yew talks, he doesn’t just trash all Americans. In fact, he goes out of his way to say: “I think there are highly intelligent, well-meaning people…” and even admits: “I’ve had three experiences with the Americans, and they did not intend any harm in each.” Keep in mind, he’s saying this while referring to the exact American CIA agents who he caught red-handed trying to bribe him!

It speaks to the dynamic I still hear about the “Ideal American,” and to be honest, wish to embody in the modern age. The “Curious American” is the American who is unafraid to ask probing, uncomfortable questions because they want to interrogate how a cultural or economic system works. “Curious American” Will is the me who looks deeply into Japanese honorifics or societal norms, asking how they developed and how they function in the modern day. It’s a hyper-focused pressure that other cultures might find off-putting initially, but usually end up enjoying once they realize it comes from genuine interest.

The “Pleasant American” is the American who is open-minded and laughs loudly, proudly, and contagiously. The “Pleasant American” Will is the Will who tells the chef that they expected to dislike the food, but specifically describes the mixture of flavors that makes chicken heart and gizzard taste so good. The ideal “American,” whom other countries love, has that kinetic, well-intentioned charm that’s hard to hate.

But in the end, Lee Kuan Yew points to the massive structural blindspot behind it. Well-meaning individuals don’t automatically make for a wise state. In the next sentence, he states: “The administration lacks depth and judgment, or wisdom which comes out of an accumulation of knowledge of human beings and human situations.”

This is the “Bad America,” the clumsy apparatus that I find myself having to clarify that I don’t represent when talking to non-Americans. The America that people are looking out for when they ask, “What do you think of Trump?” The American system that can take that bright, optimistic energy and run it through an institutional meat-grinder. It spits out a hubris that assumes every ancient, complex human situation can be solved from “first principles” with raw money and brute-force engineering.

Try to solve human culture without deep context, and you get the geopolitical equivalent of Tesla underground people movers (trains) or Klarna buy-now-pay-later (credit cards). You get a state apparatus that thinks you can train automated agents using lots of money and computing power to do a complicated job with many edge cases (humans). It turns out there are actually some problems that require deep analysis with historical empathy toward all parties involved.

After this, you will see in the video that the live interview team thought the interview was over. They start to play music and zoom out slowly, with the faces of contemplative journalists looking toward the camera team. Instead, though, Lee Kuan Yew decides to talk over the music and lands one final point, bringing up America’s most recent political failure in Cuba. His message was simple: treat complex, sovereign peoples like simple chess pieces long enough, and eventually one of them is going to snap and flip the board back on you.

Today, I believe this all still holds true. That bright, curious American spirit is still out there, even if it's currently trying to outrun our sprawling, modern political-corporate machine. For me, that’s my answer to “what do you think of Trump.” Not the shrug, but what comes after it. I take the seat right in the middle of the class, first hands up. I ask the questions that everyone else is too embarrassed to ask, and when a classmate gets stuck halfway through a sentence, I’m the one who jumps in to help reword it, unafraid of messing up. The person that no one is afraid to push to the front. That's the kind of American I still want other people to see.

Now to be more clear, the sins of the greater AI business are many. What I, in this article, would like to attempt is to explain my own personal AI usage guide and thought process.

However, I would indeed like to start by enumerating the unignorable major failures of the greater AI industry (and why you should still keep using AI individually).

Starting with the physical bedrock: the processing data centers.

A data center has real costs: its location, power draw, a bit of water (though, really not as much as the memes would have you believe), and the fact that data centers are deafeningly loud to live next to, albeit in a difficult-to-legally-indict infrasound way. However, a good data center can exist, and should exist, near where humans live.

The problem is that, well, the easiest way to build a data center is to just plop it in an overgrown, unused business park less than 200 feet from a suburban expanse, using “temporary” gas or even coal power while “the real renewable capabilities are being built.” Even worse, they are even placed in deserts that barely have the infrastructure to support the people already living there.

Which gets to the reason these data centers are being built. In reality, an individual AI query from a person, even using a pretty large context window, only comes to about 0.3 Wh per query. That is in the same ballpark as a basic web search, once you count all the ad tech that searching and scrolling multiple web pages actually has to load. Eating 100g less meat a week and turning off lights is a much more efficient way of reducing energy usage than refusing to use AI.

What isn’t efficient is the modern corporate usage of AI. What this looks like is stuff like:

* ~2.5 to 40 Wh per request trying to stuff the literal whole codebase into Claude Pro (which can happen multiple times a minute)
* ~2.5 to 3.0 Wh per request trying to make insulting renders of Miyazaki characters
* ~30 to 90+ Wh per short video clip trying to convince your boss that you have an insane new project idea

And finally, the mega-GWh training synthesis of completely new models

The cool part, though, is that all of these types of background projects don't actually require low latency. These huge data centers could feasibly be built an hour away from civilization, leaving smaller local data centers to handle real-time general AI requests and cause fewer localized problems.

So if you were to ask me, I would say building a data center nearby is fine—but when I say that, I mean 5 miles away from any living humans, surrounded by its own self-sufficient energy grid, up in the northern states where fresh water and cold weather can support the cooling naturally. In general, I would tell most people to put pressure on their local governments to properly tax and hold data centers accountable, rather than pushing for the full abolition of all data centers in the States.

The training data theft is also a huge deal that deserves its own longer article. However, in total: it happened, it cannot be undone, and at this point, the best thing to do is support your favorite artists directly and keep the real humans behind the art alive.

BUT. That was all preamble to my real topic: general AI usage, and how I use it without feeling like I’m settling for a half-powered version of 2002 Google.

What I would like to talk about is the particulars of generative text. Not overall neural networks, like the ones used for protein folding, but specifically how I think about text chatbots and how I find them useful.

First off: what exactly is generative AI? Though this is constantly talked about, I have my own way of thinking about it that might be helpful and differentiate generative AI from actual computing.

The way generative text works starts with breaking all of human language into chunks of letters called tokens—sometimes full words like “the” or “like”, and sometimes word fragments like “anti” or “pre”. The parameters (the 10B param models) are the 10 billion numerical weights that calculate how closely related all those letter chunks are to each other across the training data. When you execute a query, the system builds a running vector guided by these weights. (This is why hearing “open weights” means giving users access to how tuned these relationships are.) After taking into context all the vectors coming from the different word groupings, it adds them up into a probability spread to generate the next letter chunk.

It does get a lot more complicated, don't get me wrong, but in general, it is still just vectors trying to look like thinking. That same probability spread results in the “same words, different reply” phenomenon. It makes it feel like the AI is doing original thinking, but in reality, it's just the same vector choosing a different route due to a different probability seed. The same goes for new models with a “reasoning layer,” which makes it sound like it's doing human thinking and double-checking; in reality, it just continues generating on that same path and writes its scratchpad down first. Overall, thinking of AI models as a summation of vectors pointing to the most likely next group of characters is the most helpful way to view them.

In this way, what LLMs are trying to do is figure out what grouping of letters you are trying to find, and then finish your thought. Because of that, where AI sucks is in low-context queries using non-vernacular terms. Where AI is best is when a question is offered with full situational context using specific language, because that brings the vector quickly into the area you are trying to find, pointing directly toward the word connections in that context.

Why this matters can best be seen in some basic examples of how old computing worked versus how modern AI systems handle the same task.

When we talk about how modern LLMs suck as a Google stand-in, it comes down to the difference between retrieving data from a worldwide database versus inferring an answer and looking for inferential confirmation.

Old Google, as us old millennials used to know and love, was an algorithm that directly matched your words and used a massive page-ranking system to show you which page was clicked most often. It was a system that demanded extreme specificity in literal term matching, relying on the wisdom of the crowd to highlight the websites people actually liked!

For example, say you were looking for places to go on a classic Japan trip. A pro "Google-fu" search would look like:

```travel "anime pilgrimage" OR "vibe" Kyoto -news -tourist -top10```

As you can see, if you could find that magic word or phrase people often used, you could find exactly what you wanted and filter out overused results.

Or take another example. I know it's overused, but I use it for that exact reason. Say you want to cook with leftover ingredients and exclude things you don’t have or straight-up dislike:

```"sichuan peppercorns" "soft tofu" "sesame oil" recipe -peanut -fish```

It’s a direct list that finds the most popular recipe fitting your exact criteria. But in the end, the page you click was human-approved by the internet populace.

Let's compare that to modern Gemini/Google searching. The main problem people walk into is that the output feels wrong—though technically it isn't, because modern AI requires input context above all else. Some classic recent examples include asking “Is [X] show available in my region?”, which yields a "yes" (without clarifying it was only available on one niche site for one specific year), or “What is buying the dip?”, which provides recipes for delicious guacamole and nacho dips (rather than explaining the common WallStreetBets financial phrase). In both cases, the AI was technically correct based on raw string matching. The issue is that modern AI makes a closest-match inference off your text input and searches using that. Page ranking now plays a secondary role.

Say we go back to those two previous examples. The proper way to search them "AI-style" is:

I am planning a 2-day stop in Kyoto. It is my third time visiting. I dislike crowded spots (e.g., Fushimi Inari, Arashiyama bamboo grove) and this time I’d like to visit some anime pilgrimage sites, or just some places with chill vibes

```Generate a 2-day itinerary with: Hourly timeline for morning/afternoon blocks. Transit time estimates between locations. Explicit "Anti-Recommendations" (places right next to these spots that I should skip due to tourist volume). Format as a Markdown table.```

Or the recipe example:

```I recently bought way too much sichuan peppercorns, and I’d like to make a dish that uses that, along with soft tofu, sesame oil, rice vinegar, chicken broth, and chili flakes. I do NOT like: peanuts, or fish dishes (especially fish oil). I’m especially done with all these firm tofu recipes. Like ohmygosh. Act as a home chef. Create a 15-minute stir-fry or soup recipe using ONLY the ingredients listed above plus basic salt and pepper. Explain the step-by-step recipe and common mistake points.```

And yes, I did, in fact, use AI to create an AI prompt. That is often the best way to ensure the AI has the full context it needs.

In both cases, knowing the structure of what you are searching for is how you perform a helpful query. But what AI offers as an advantage is that it helps you reach that experienced level much faster.

Take another example: trying to figure out how to fix a wobbly backyard railing. An experienced DIYer will tell you to “just look up a YouTube video.” What they don't realize is that, precisely because they are experienced, they already know how to parse that video. They skim straight to a few frames, identify the tools needed, and understand the core mechanism. For a complete novice, that's where AI shines.

You start with a simple, conversational query: “I’ve got a deck railing that’s kind of wobbly. Can you help me figure out how to tackle this, including tools, troubleshooting steps, and what to look for?”

By providing your general goal, the AI takes your plain language and points its vector space toward the correct technical terminology, common tools, and structural approaches.

From there, you get up to speed much faster without spending money messing up first. Once you have those terms, you can do a targeted search for the specific tools or inspect the railing yourself—which finally gets you to the point where you can watch that YouTube video and actually understand it.

The problem is that AI marketing has been filled with false promises from the start, pretending that using AI requires zero human skill. In reality, using old Google and YouTube required learned parsing skills too; it's just that back then, those tools weren't being aggressively pushed into every corporate workflow overnight.

This is why the current marketing push, trying to frame AI as an all-knowing personal assistant that manages your daily life, fails pathetically. Reliably automating personal errands requires deterministic, real-time context about every variable in your life. Furthermore, human daily life isn't a programming script with clean success/failure states, making full automation virtually impossible.

Ultimately, I use generative AI to convert what I don’t know into what I need to ask next by translating my loose ideas into precise, domain-specific language.

Finally, I want to touch on why AI-generated code, while functional on the surface, can be a major headache for experienced software engineers.

As any CS major will tell you, developers rarely build a modern codebase entirely from scratch. Historically, they go to places like StackOverflow to find clean, modular snippets designed for specific problems. This mirrors the old Google model: finding robust, human-verified code and integrating it deliberately.

AI does not work like this. It takes a plain-language request and generates a probability vector pointing toward a whole cloud of similar-looking code across its training data. The result is a hodgepodge: workable, but often bloated, oddly structured, and awkwardly commented. It isn't solving the engineering problem; it is pattern-matching toward something that resembles a solution.

And that is how we end up with massive Claude Pro bills and sprawling data center demands. It uses a high-dimensional vector engine to guess at syntax, consuming 40 Wh per complex request for a task the ~0.2 Wh human brain handles deterministically.

We are currently stuck in a corporate landscape where generating raw output volume is conflated with productivity (if we go by the promises of executives like Sam Altman or Mark Zuckerberg). I’m not saying people forced to use AI for bloated corporate benchmarks should lose their internet privileges—but it is why I stay firmly on the side of normal, low-impact brainstorming requests. Let probability vectors do what they are actually good at, instead of pretending to be something they aren't.
